## What this course covers

This course helps managers make better investment and financing decisions in
entrepreneurial settings, exploring topics from both entrepreneur and investor
perspectives. It focuses on the venture capital process, covering the full lifecycle of
a new venture from deal sourcing and evaluation to term sheets, fund performance, and
exits. The course uses a hybrid model combining asynchronous online lectures and
readings with synchronous, interactive case discussions and exercises.

### MGTF 410 New Venture Finance (Townsend) WI26

New Venture Finance
PROFESSOR: Richard Townsend
EMAIL: rrtownsend@ucsd.edu
PHONE: (858) 246-1632
OFFICE: Otterson Hall 4S153

DESCRIPTION
This course is designed to help managers make better investment and financing decisions in
entrepreneurial settings. Aimed at students interested in working as either entrepreneurs or
investors, the class will explore topics from both perspectives. The curriculum focuses on the
intersection of entrepreneurship and finance, with a primary emphasis on the venture capital
process. We will cover the full lifecycle of a new venture, from sourcing and evaluating deals to
structuring term sheets, measuring fund performance, and planning for a successful exit. The
course uses a hybrid instructional model, combining asynchronous online lectures and readings
with synchronous, interactive case discussions and exercises.
OBJECTIVES
At the close of this course, you will be able to:
• Analyze investment opportunities by evaluating the four key areas of a startup: the
founding team, market opportunity, product, and early traction.
• Calculate startup valuations using the VC Method, working backward from a projected
exit value while accounting for target returns and future dilution.
• Structure and negotiate venture financing deals by interpreting the key economic and
control terms in term sheets, such as convertible preferred stock, anti-dilution clauses,
and protective provisions.
• Evaluate the structure, economics, and performance of venture capital funds from the
perspective of both a General Partner (GP) and a Limited Partner (LP).
• Compare and contrast various funding sources for new ventures, including venture
capital, angel investing, crowdfunding, and venture debt.
• Develop an optimal exit strategy for a startup by evaluating the trade-offs between the
two primary exit avenues: an Initial Public Offering (IPO) and an acquisition.

REQUIREMENTS
Homework assignments or case memos will be due at the beginning of most class meetings.
Each case will come with guideline questions. Students are required to submit a two-page
memorandum answering the questions prior to class. You may prepare the case memo in a group
and submit only one copy, but no group may exceed 3 students.
In your memos and in class discussions, you should frame your arguments to win business
discussions, not academic arguments. Academic research can give context to your ideas, but
should not be the central focus of your memo or comments in class. You are looking to convince
your colleagues and the instructor that your business proposal should be followed.
The case-portion of this course makes class participation a critical component of the learning
process. It is important that you attend every class and come prepared to add to the discussion.
You will be graded on the quality of your argument and presentation. It will be important to
convince others of your positions, while always relating to classmates in a respectful manner. In
the class, as in the business world, communication skills will weigh heavily in your evaluation.
The final exam in the course will be on an individual basis.

CHATGPT AND OTHER AI TOOLS
Large Language Models and AI in general are new and powerful tools. They can be helpful for
asking questions about course material to deepen your understanding, but they can also make
mistakes. As these tools are relatively new, this policy is designed to provide clear guidelines on
how they can be used productively and ethically in this course.
You are encouraged to use AI tools as a learning aid. For instance, you can use them to:
• Define and explain key concepts from the lectures and readings.
• Ask for alternative examples of topics discussed in class.
• Get help understanding complex financial models or terms.
However, you must ultimately complete the graded work on your own. You should not enter
assignment questions directly into AI tools to get answers. Submitting work generated by an
AI tool as your own is a violation of academic integrity. For assignments, you are expected to do
the analysis and write-up yourself.
The midterm and final exams will emphasize your ability to understand and implement the
analytical tools discussed in this course. You are expected to work on your own, without the
assistance of AI tools, on all exams.
