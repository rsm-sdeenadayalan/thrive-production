# MGT 430 — Biotechnology Industry, Structure & Strategy

**4 units** · Rady MBA · offered SU26

## What this course covers

This course examines the biotechnology industry as a business, focusing on running a
biotechnology company rather than starting one. It combines discussion and lecture with
real-world case studies, covering major functional areas including discovery,
development, manufacturing, regulation, marketing, and sales, as well as strategy,
financing, licensing, intellectual property, reimbursement, and organizational behavior.
Students take leadership roles in case discussions and complete an in-depth company
analysis.

## What you should be able to do afterwards

- analyzing biotechnology company business models and competitive positioning
- evaluating strategic options and trade-offs under uncertainty
- leading case discussions and defending recommendations
- conducting primary research through interviews and public sources
- assessing financial impact of strategic decisions
- developing actionable recommendations from a senior executive perspective

## Topics covered

biotechnology industry structure, drug discovery and development, manufacturing, regulation, marketing and sales, strategy, financing, licensing and intellectual property, reimbursement, bioethics and organizational behavior.

## Tools used

canvas.

None stated in the syllabus.

Class participation and online assignments count 35% and the Company Assignment paper counts 65%; final grades reflect absolute performance and relative standing, with no exams.

COURSE DESCRIPTION AND GOALS
Biotechnology started as a science, referring to the use of living cells as factories to produce
protein through manipulation of genes. However, today biotechnology refers to an industry, with
the top companies in the sector exceeding some of the major pharmaceutical companies in
market capitalization. No longer are biotechnology companies constrained to using recombinant
DNA technology alone, as the moniker is now assigned to any small company engaged in life
sciences-related research directed toward developing a commercial product using a wide range
of scientific approaches.
These products are not necessarily limited to therapeutics but may include diagnostic or medical
device products. Belonging to the sector also implies a culture—small, nimble, visionary yet
practical, cash-constrained, and willing to take significant risks. While some of these
characteristics are idealized, it is clear that although development-stage companies rely on the
same scientific, analytical, and managerial talent found in large pharmaceutical and device
companies, the context is fundamentally different. Success requires additional skills,
adaptability, and non-traditional systems.
The foundation of this course builds on the core curriculum you have completed during your first
year at Rady. You will have an advantage relative to many industry professionals in your ability
to apply structured thinking and analysis.
However, the industry presents unique challenges. Long development cycles can reduce
urgency in decision-making, and the ability to operate at a loss for extended periods can distort
incentives. In some cases, promotion may take precedence over rigorous analysis, and shortterm share price considerations may overshadow long-term value creation.
This course is designed to avoid these traps. You will be expected to think across functions,
adapt to changing conditions, and operate with limited margin for error. Your personal decisionmaking style will be tested in a highly visible and dynamic environment.

COURSE FORMAT
The goal of this course is not to make you experts on industry facts and figures. Instead, the
objective is to teach you to identify issues, rigorously analyze them, and develop actionable
solutions from the perspective of a senior manager in a biotechnology enterprise.
The course focuses on running a biotechnology company rather than starting one. It combines
discussion and lecture, anchored around real-world case studies.
You will be exposed to all major functional areas, including discovery, development,
manufacturing, regulation, marketing, and sales. Broader topics such as strategy, financing,
licensing, intellectual property, bioethics, reimbursement, and organizational behavior will also
be covered.
The course includes three in-person Saturday sessions and online modules. In-person sessions
will feature guest lecturers and student-led case discussions. Sessions run from 1:00 pm to 5:00

Given the breadth of content and limited time, some prioritization is required. While medical
devices and diagnostics will be discussed, the primary focus will be on pharmaceutical
applications due to their complexity.
Students will frequently take on leadership roles in discussions. Additional formats may include
team exercises and guest speakers.
Throughout the course, you are expected to evaluate situations based on the information
available at the time and make decisions from the perspective of a senior executive
operating under real-world constraints.

investors.
All students are expected to actively participate in all case discussions, regardless of whether
they are part of the presenting team.

PREPARATION AND EXPECTATIONS
Students are expected to read and prepare all assigned cases in advance of each session.
For those presenting during the first session, an outline on how to structure the case discussion
will be provided in Rady Canvas. This outline is intended to guide your analysis and clarify
expectations.
Students are encouraged to reach out in advance with any questions.
As outlined in the course bibliography, there is a significant amount of assigned reading in
addition to the cases. Required readings are identified in black and recommended readings in
blue. Additional materials may be provided throughout the course.
Students are expected to determine which information is most relevant to addressing the key
issues in each case. You should approach each case as a senior executive responsible for
making decisions, not as a passive reviewer of information.
This means:
• Identifying the critical issues
• Evaluating trade-offs across potential solutions
• Developing a clear and actionable plan
Students are encouraged to go beyond the case materials and incorporate external sources,
including company websites, public filings, and discussions with individuals who have relevant
experience.
You will be evaluated based on problem-solving and decision-making abilities rather than
memorization of content. Consistent with this philosophy, there are no exams in this course.
CASE EVALUATION FRAMEWORK
Students must evaluate each case within the context of the time period in which it was written.
You should not rely on information, outcomes, or solutions that became available after the case
timeframe.
This is not an exercise in hindsight. Rather, you are expected to assess the situation based on
what was known or reasonably knowable at that time.
You should approach each case as if you are making decisions in real time under uncertainty.
The objective is to determine what you would do if you were in their position, given the
information, constraints, and uncertainties that existed at that point.
These assignments are designed to reinforce analytical thinking, independent judgment,
and the ability to connect strategic decisions to real-world outcomes, including financial
impact.

COMPANY ASSIGNMENT (65% OF FINAL GRADE)
The remaining 65% of your grade will be based on a Company Assignment paper submitted in
three installments over the duration of the quarter, with the final installment due the evening of
the last session.
The objective of this assignment is to apply course concepts through the in-depth analysis of a
life sciences company. Each student will select a company with which they have no more than
a passing familiarity. This is an individual assignment, and each student must select a unique
company. A sign-up sheet will be available on Rady Canvas to confirm selections.
Students are expected to utilize both publicly available information and primary research
through interviews. At least two interviews must be conducted with individuals connected to the
company or its ecosystem. These may include company executives, competitors, industry
analysts, academic experts, or other relevant stakeholders.
Interviews must be based solely on publicly available information and general industry
perspectives. Students should not seek or request any confidential, proprietary, or non-public
information. Under no circumstances should a student request or agree to sign a non-disclosure
agreement (NDA) as part of this assignment. This is not an exercise in obtaining confidential
information, but in applying analytical thinking to publicly available data and informed
perspectives.
This is not a descriptive report, but an analytical assessment supported by evidence. Students
are expected to form independent perspectives that may differ from company messaging or
public narratives.
Students should leverage high-quality sources where available, including SEC filings, earnings
calls, investor presentations, scientific publications, and other credible materials. Interview
insights must be integrated into the analysis and not presented as a separate section.
INSTALLMENT 1 – COMPANY PROFILE
Provide a comprehensive overview of the company, including:
• Products and/or services
• Target markets and customers
• Competitive positioning
• Financial and operational performance
The goal is to establish a clear understanding of the company’s business model, strategy, and
current state.

5

INSTALLMENT 2 – KEY ISSUES OR OPPORTUNITIES
Identify three distinct strategic issues or opportunities facing the company.
These must be:
• Specific and company-relevant
• Actionable and decision-oriented
• Grounded in the company’s unique context, including its technology, leadership, market
dynamics, and investor landscape
Broad or generic industry trends are not sufficient unless clearly tied to company-specific
impact.
Students must:
• Clearly define each issue or opportunity
• Prioritize the three items
• Provide a rationale for the ranking
After prioritization, select one issue or opportunity for deeper analysis. This does not need to be
the top-ranked item, but the choice should be justified.
INSTALLMENT 3 – FINAL ANALYSIS AND RECOMMENDATION
Evaluate how the company is currently addressing the selected issue or opportunity.
Based on your research and interviews:
• Assess the effectiveness of the company’s approach
• Identify gaps, risks, or assumptions in their strategy
• Provide your own perspective on how the issue or opportunity should be addressed
• Assess the potential financial impact of both the company’s approach and your
recommended alternative, including implications on revenue, cost structure, profitability,
valuation, or market positioning where applicable
Students should clearly state whether they agree or disagree with the company’s approach and
support their position with data, analysis, and interview insights.
Your recommendation should reflect how a senior executive or investor would evaluate and act
on this issue, including the expected financial implications of that decision.
Additional assignment details will be provided on Rady Canvas.

MODULE 7: LICENSING (Video Lecture)
For most early-stage biotechnology companies, the likelihood that they will see the
development of their product through to commercialization as a stand-alone company is slight.
On the other side of the coin, large pharmaceutical, device and biotechnology companies are

new products. Licensing, therefore, is of critical importance to any and all players in the

MODULE 9: PRICING
The prior session on Health Economics and Market Access provides a natural segue into a
discussion on pricing of new healthcare technologies, through the analysis of the economic
value of the product. Pricing has the single biggest impact on the return on investment from a
new healthcare technology and getting the launch price right is of critical importance, as the
ability to increase the price of a pharmaceutical product after launch is significantly constrained.
Additionally, the issue of pricing of new healthcare technologies in the current environment of
significant resource constraints brings forth much discussion about the ethics of drug pricing.
While there will be no time to devote specifically to the important issue of bioethics, this topic
will likely find its way into many of our class discussions, in particular the session on pricing.
There are no specific articles or texts in the reading list on this topic but those who are interested
in this topic may make a request for suggestions.

MODULE 3: HEALTH TECHNOLOGY REGULATION:
The biotechnology industry is very heavily regulated and understanding how best to navigate
the maze of regulation through the various stages of development to commercialization is a key
strategic capability. A knowledgeable and experienced regulatory affairs team can shave
significant time, effort and investment off the development process and thereby significantly
improve a company’s return on investment.
