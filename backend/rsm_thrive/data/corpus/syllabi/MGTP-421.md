# MGTP 421 — Internal Control over Financial Reporting

**4 units** · Rady MPAc (Accountancy) · offered FA26

## What this course covers

Internal control over financial reporting (ICFR) and its practical application within
business processes that produce financial information. The course covers management's
and independent auditors' perspectives, including financial reporting assertions, risks
of material misstatement, control objectives, control activities, and evaluation of
control deficiencies, along with IT controls, governance, fraud risk, and emerging
technologies. It is taught from a professional and practical perspective with case
studies, guest speakers, group presentations, and documentation exercises.

## What you should be able to do afterwards

- explaining icfr and the coso framework
- analyzing transaction flows through major business processes
- identifying financial reporting assertions and risks of material misstatement
- evaluating control objectives and control design
- assessing control deficiencies
- documenting processes, risks, and controls with professional tools
- explaining the role of it controls and emerging technologies in icfr
- communicating icfr analyses and conclusions effectively

## Topics covered

internal control over financial reporting and coso framework, business process documentation, purchasing and payroll cycle, inventory and production cycle, sales and collection cycle, general accounting and month-end close, it controls and emerging technologies, corporate governance and audit committees, fraud risk and control deficiencies.

## Tools used

power bi, canvas, process narratives, systems flowcharts, data flow diagrams, risk-control matrices, entity relationship diagrams, generative ai.

COURSE OVERVIEW AND JUSTIFICATION:
Internal control over financial reporting (“ICFR”) is fundamental to the preparation of reliable financial statements and
to the effective operation of an organization. This course introduces students to the practical application of ICFR
concepts within the business processes that produce financial information. It is designed for students who may have
studied auditing but have not previously examined ICFR as a distinct professional discipline.
The course approaches ICFR from both the company’s and the independent auditor’s perspectives. Students consider
how management designs and operate controls to prevent or detect errors and fraud, how companies evaluate whether
controls address identified risks, and how independent auditors consider the design and operating effectiveness of
controls in the context of their responsibilities. The course emphasizes the connection among financial reporting
assertions, risks of material misstatement, control objectives, control activities, and the evaluation of control
deficiencies.
Students apply these concepts to the purchasing and payroll, inventory and production, sales and collection, and general
accounting and month-end/close processes. They analyze how transactions originate, move through an organization, are
recorded in accounting systems, and ultimately affect the financial statements. Students learn to document processes
and controls using professional tools, including process narratives, systems flowcharts, data flow diagrams, risk-control
matrices, and entity relationship diagrams, while maintaining appropriate focus on the practical purpose of each tool.
The course also considers the role of information technology, IT general and application controls, corporate governance,
audit committees, internal auditing, fraud and error risk, and risk management in supporting reliable financial reporting.
A portion of the course addresses how automated systems, data analytics, and artificial intelligence may affect the
design, implementation, operation, and assessment of ICFR. These topics are considered in relation to financial reporting
risk and control—not as a comprehensive information systems or technology course.
The course is taught from a professional and practical perspective. Students are expected to engage with substantial
professional and other assigned reading, exercise judgment, support conclusions with appropriate analysis, and
communicate with the level of care expected of individuals beginning their professional careers. The course also
provides the basis and knowledge of ICFR in relevant preparation for the CPA examination, while maintaining a broader
professional objective: preparing students to participate meaningfully in the design, implementation, documentation,
evaluation, assessment and improvement of ICFR.

FOCUS:
The course focuses on the relationship between business processes and reliable financial reporting. For each major
process, students consider:
•

the nature and flow of transactions and related financial information;

•

the financial statement accounts and assertions affected by the process;

•

the risks of material misstatement arising from the process;

•

the objectives that appropriately respond to those risks;

•

the controls designed and implemented to achieve those objectives;

•

the information technology and other systems that support the process;

•

the evidence necessary to understand and evaluate the controls; and

•

the nature and implications of any identified control deficiencies.

The principal processes addressed are purchasing and payroll, inventory and production, sales and collection, and
general accounting and month-end reporting. Documentation is treated as both a professional communication skill and
a means of analyzing whether a process and its controls are adequately designed.
The course further examines ICFR in the broader organizational context, including the COSO framework, corporate
governance, audit committees, internal auditing, fraud risk, and the relationship between management’s responsibilities
and the independent auditor’s responsibilities. IT controls and emerging technologies are addressed insofar as they
affect financial reporting risk and the reliability of information used in control activities and financial reporting. The
course is not intended to provide a comprehensive treatment of auditing procedures or information systems design;
rather, it establishes the ICFR foundation necessary for students to understand and evaluate controls in professional
practice.
COURSE OUTCOMES:
Upon successful completion of the course, students will be able to:
1. Explain ICFR and the COSO framework.
2. Explain the roles of management, governance, internal audit, and the independent auditor.
3. Analyze the flow of transactions and financial information through major business processes.
4. Identify relevant assertions and risks of material misstatement.
5. Evaluate control objectives and control design.
6. Identify and assess control deficiencies.
7. Document processes, risks, and controls using professional tools.
8. Explain the role of IT controls and emerging technologies, including AI, in ICFR.
9. Explain the relationship between ICFR and financial statement auditing, including PCAOB AS 2201.
10. Consider the costs, benefits, limitations, and feasibility of control alternatives.
11. Communicate ICFR analyses and conclusions effectively in writing and presentations.

PARTICIPATION:
You are expected to be prepared for class, constructively participate in class discussions and contribute to your group
project.

QUIZZES:
Each class will start with a brief in-person closed-note, closed-book quiz that covers (1) the lecture content from the prior
class and (2) the readings assigned and due for that class. These quizzes are not expected to be tricky or exceedingly
challenging; rather, meant to simply ensure that you are reading the Assignments and reviewing the prior week’s notes.
You may drop the lowest score.
SPEAKER ADVANCED PREP.:
In advance of a guest joining our class there will be an individual assignment to complete. The assignment will include
you researching the speaker’s experiences and accomplishments. Your findings will be posted to a class discussion board
along with topics you would like addressed by our guest as well as questions you have for the speaker.
SPEAKER REFLECTIONS:
Following presentations by our Guest you will be asked to submit a short paper (not to exceed 200 words). The paper
should include your reflection on what you heard and learned from the speaker(s).
GROUP PROJECT:
The group project will be created at the intersection of Internal Controls, Financial Reporting and Business Risks. In
summary, students will work within small groups to prepare a short presentation to the class and lead a discussion
addressing either a fraud related situation or reported Material Weakness with the specific subject area being decided on
by each group. There will also be a brief presentation leveraging Power BI. More detailed project requirements will be
provided in class and posted to Canvas.
FINAL EXAM:
Students will be given a closed-note, closed-book exam which will ensure that they have achieved the learning objectives
of the course. The internal control over financial reporting course is conceptual rather than numerical/calculation-based,
so it is more appropriate that the Final Exam be closed note, closed book. This also more closely simulates the
requirements for the CPA examination.
USE OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE (“AI”)
This policy applies to the use of various generative AI tools that can generate text, images, etc., in response to prompts
by users (e.g., ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Claude, Perplexity AI, etc.). You are permitted to use these tools for this class,
with several caveats and conditions:
1. These tools should augment your work, not replace it. These tools can help you get started on ideas; help overcome
writer’s block or refine YOUR original ideas. These tools should not be used to generate content that you simply put your
name on. This constitutes an academic integrity violation, much like taking another individual’s work and
misrepresenting it as your own original work (see end of syllabus for details on UCSD’s academic integrity policy).
2. If you use generative AI for an assignment, be transparent about if/how it was used in a footnote or citation. Using
generative AI tools without attributing credit will be treated as comparable to failing to properly credit and
cite/reference other people’s contributions and sources used in your work.
3. I will assume all students have access to and will use Generative AI and LLMs. If your work does not exceed the
baseline quality level produced by AI, you should expect a lower grade.

4. Large language models have documented issues with hallucination and inaccuracy (e.g., generating fake citations,
failing logical arguments, misrepresenting sources, etc.). You are fully responsible for the content you submit in your
assignments and will be graded accordingly.
