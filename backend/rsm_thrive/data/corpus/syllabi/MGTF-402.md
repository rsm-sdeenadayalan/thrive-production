# MGTF 402 — Investment Analysis

**4 units** · Rady MQF (Master of Quantitative Finance) · offered FA26

## What this course covers

This course develops a framework for understanding how investors choose between risky
and risk-free securities and construct optimal portfolios. It covers asset pricing
models such as CAPM and factor models, fixed income analysis, derivatives, and
performance evaluation, with an emphasis on practical implementation using data and
cases. Topics also include ESG investing, market efficiency, behavioral finance, private
markets, and the use of AI in asset management.

## What you should be able to do afterwards

- constructing efficient portfolios
- estimating factor models
- evaluating fund performance
- pricing bonds and managing bond portfolios
- pricing options using binomial lattice and black-scholes
- conducting event studies
- implementing asset allocation strategies
- measuring risk and return

## Topics covered

risk and return, modern portfolio theory, asset allocation, capm and factor models, market efficiency and behavioral finance, performance evaluation, fixed income analysis, term structure of interest rates, options and derivatives, private markets and esg investing.

COURSE OBJECTIVES
Building on the idea of a risk-return trade-off, this course develops a framework for understanding how investors
choose between risky and risk-free financial securities and determine which combinations of assets are desirable.
This is the asset allocation or portfolio decision. Finance is inherently a practitioner's science and we emphasize the
practical implementation of asset allocation methods: How do we go from the theory to compute actual numbers
using a combination of data and subjective judgment? The aim is to provide experience in applying techniques in
common use in the portfolio management industry.
A better understanding of portfolio choice requires knowledge of which components of returns can be diversified
and which cannot. It also requires understanding how risk premiums are determined, how well they can be estimated
and how stable they are through time. To this end we turn to asset pricing models, including the Capital Asset
Pricing Model (CAPM) and develop factor pricing models that characterize risk in terms of securities' exposures to a
range of common sources of risk, including macroeconomic risk factors. This also leads to the idea of style investing
-- accounting for value, growth and size effects -- which is covered in some detail.
How can the techniques of modern portfolio theory help us understand the impact of Environmental, Social, and
Governance (ESG) investing? Will pursuing ESG investing reduce portfolio returns or does it, in contrast, hold the
promise of better future performance?
Is money left on the table and are our investment models any good in practice? To discuss this, we turn to market
efficiency and behavioral finance. We will introduce tools to test market efficiency using so-called event study
methodology. If anyone can outperform, presumably it is professional fund managers. We therefore study mutual
funds and hedge funds and discuss how to evaluate their performance on a risk-adjusted basis using the asset pricing
models developed in the previous part of the course. We discuss recent trends in FinTech towards using AI and
algorithms for automated investment decisions.
The course also introduces private markets (private equity, venture capital, and private credit) as an increasingly
important allocation for institutional and, increasingly, individual investors, and considers digital assets
(cryptocurrencies) as a candidate asset class, evaluating diversification claims against the empirical evidence.
The course next turns to bond pricing, including how to understand the relationship between interest rates at
different maturities (the term structure of interest rates), how to measure the risk of a single bond and how to
manage the risk of a bond portfolio. How information can be extracted from the term structure of interest rates will
also be discussed.
The final topic covered in the course is derivative securities such as options, forwards and futures. We characterize
their payoffs, look at their pricing, including binomial lattice methods and the Black-Scholes option pricing formula
and discuss how they can be used in investment strategies.
The course engages participants in the empirical analysis of a variety of financial data sets. While most of the
assignments can be done in Excel, knowledge of programming languages such as Python is beneficial but not
required.
