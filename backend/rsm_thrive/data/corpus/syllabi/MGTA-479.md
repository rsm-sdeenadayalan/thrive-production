# MGTA 479 — Pricing Analytics

**4 units** · MSBA (Rady MS Business Analytics) · offered WI26

## What this course covers

A heavily quantitative, hands-on course treating pricing as the intersection of
economics, strategy, and data science. Covers value pricing, demand estimation from
surveys, experiments, and field data, price discrimination, bundling, and subscriptions,
applied to real managerial data with a team pricing-strategy project for an actual
product or business.

## What you should be able to do afterwards

- quantifying customer value
- estimating demand and setting optimal prices
- analyzing pricing data in Python
- designing a data-driven pricing strategy
- presenting pricing recommendations

## Topics covered

value pricing, demand estimation, survey methods, pricing experiments, price discrimination, bundling and subscriptions, sales promotions.

## Tools used

Python.

## Prerequisites

Statistics and regression knowledge expected; no prior economics needed.

## How it is graded

10% homework, 10% attendance/participation, 30% midterm, 50% team project.

## Workload

Moderate. Five short homeworks, in-class midterm, and a substantial team project worth half the grade.

DESCRIPTION
Price setting is one of the most crucial of all tactical marketing decisions. It involves a detailed
understanding of both supply side (e.g. costs) and demand side factors (e.g., consumer willingness to
pay). Traditional approaches to pricing have revolved around an economic and financial framework. A
broader and more pragmatic view entails a comprehensive understanding of the demand side, both at the
level of individual customer values and the aggregate level of price sensitivities of the market. In this
course, we will approach the pricing decision as an intersection of economic, strategic, and data science.
The course is heavily quantitative in nature. This class is practical and hands-on. Most of the data we will
analyze is from real-world managerial problems. I expect everyone to have a knowledge of statistics and
regressions. I do not expect prior knowledge of economics before the class and will cover fundamental
economic concepts in this class.
OBJECTIVES
The specific goals of this course are for you to:
• Develop an understanding of the theory and techniques of pricing
• Apply the value pricing framework to calculate expected value to customers
• Learn methods for estimating demand and selecting a price
• Understand the relevance of tactical issues in pricing and sales promotions
• Apply the theory and analysis tools in executing a pricing strategy

ASSIGNMENTS
Homeworks (10%)
There will be a number of short homework assignments due, combining conceptual questions with coding
exercises to foster your data analysis skills. In accordance with the rest of the MSBA program, we will use
Python for all coding needs. Responsible use of AI coding tools is encouraged (see course policies below
for additional details).
Class Attendance and Participation (10%)
The classroom will be an active learning environment. Students are expected to attend class and
contribute to discussions. If you have legitimate reasons preventing you from attending a class, please
email me as early as possible. Legitimate reasons can include urgent medical needs, job interviews, and
university-sanctioned extracurriculars.
Midterm (30%)
The in-class midterm is intended to test your retention of the key concepts and analytical frameworks in
the class.
Class Project (50%)
This class will have a team-based project. You should form a group of two to four people to work on
group projects. You and your team will study a particular product or business to identify critical drivers of
the pricing decision, study current pricing practices, and then make specific recommendations for the
business to improve on some element of the pricing strategy. Please schedule a meeting with the
professor as soon as your group has decided a project to study.
Teams will present their findings in the last week of class. The structure of a project presentation and
rigorous arguments are of utmost importance for a successful analysis. Each student will be required to
provide feedback on their classmates’ videos.
As in real life, there may not be sufficient information in the project (e.g. may not know precise cost data).
However, you need to develop a logical, self-consistent action plan based on the information/data (you
must get the best available approximate measure of the cost data).

Suggested structure:
1. Diagnosis: What is the main business problem the firm is facing? The front end should not be a
summary of all project facts. Provide a diagnostic overview of the major issue(s) that the company
faces. For this, you will have to provide a little background about the company/product you are
studying (including current pricing). This usually involves understanding the complication at hand and
identifying the key pricing question you should address. This means that you will have to cut through
the clutter and focus.
2. Proposed Solution: What do you recommend to solve this problem? Give the bird’s eye view of your
proposed solution. Highlight the main drivers of impact, focus on the strategy rather than the exact
implementation. Do not just say that the product ABCD should be priced at $399. Give a little more
context of the main drivers that led you to that decisions
3. Rationale: Why is this proposed solution the best among alternative solutions? This part is the heart
of the project analysis, where you convince upper management. Present your analysis and develop a
coherent pricing strategy. Support your analysis with data and reasonable assumptions. Do not wish
problems away, make your assumptions clear. Present an understanding of pros/cons of other
possible strategies, be balanced.
Occasionally there are issues of “free-riding” within a group. Please try to resolve these issues within your
group and if this fails then please contact me. If a group member is not pulling their fair share on an
assignment, I encourage you to address the issue immediately. Early intervention can typically the matter
and avoid escalation.
COURSE POLICIES
Large Language Models and other Generative AI tools
There is no doubt that LLMs and GenAI have already proven to be massively useful productivity tools.
Nevertheless, like other productivity tools they still have their drawbacks, and a skilled user will make
much better use of them than an unskilled user. However, in the context of a classroom, where problems
are necessarily simplified for pedagogical purposes, it is tempting to use them in a hands-off manner to
deal with assigned work with little human intervention. This can produce satisfactory results, but at the
expense of student learning. However, this approach will not scale well to bigger, real world problems,
where LLMs still require a human in the loop for best results. Thus, for the sake of students’ long-run
professional career, the class policy is that responsible, human-in-the-loop usage of LLMs and GenAI
tools is encouraged. There are few reliable ways to ensure this style of usage, which is why I must
instead appeal to your own long-run self-interest.
Examples of responsible, productive use:
- Working through a coding exercise in collaboration with an LLM by requesting it to write specific
functions in terms of the desired inputs and outputs, and carefully checking the LLM’s code to make
sure the human and LLM are on the same page at every step.
- Using the deep research function of an LLM to create comprehensive background reports on multiple
firms being considered for the class project. Can also be used to search for available data.
Examples of irresponsible use:
- Feeding homework questions to an LLM and submitting its answers directly without critical
examination or iteration.
- Feeding the prompt of this syllabus and asking an LLM to complete the class project for you. This will
likely yield poor, uncreative results.
In general, you should try to maximize your value-added on top of the AI tools.
