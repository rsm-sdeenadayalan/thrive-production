# MGT 458R — Experiments in Organizations

**4 units** · Rady MBA · offered WI26, WI27

## What this course covers

Businesses increasingly use experimentation in pricing, marketing, employee incentives,
and consumer behavior. This course teaches students how to design and run randomized
experiments in organizations, incorporating insights from behavioral economics. It
combines lectures with workshopping to develop experimental proposals and pilot studies.

## What you should be able to do afterwards

- designing randomized experiments
- incorporating behavioral economics insights into interventions
- running pilot studies
- analyzing experimental data
- presenting experimental proposals to decision makers
- evaluating experiments from academic literature
- providing constructive peer feedback

## Topics covered

experimental design, randomization, causal inference, behavioral economics, pilot studies, ethics of experimentation, communicating experimental results, culture of experimentation, field experiments, online experiments.

## Tools used

qualtrics, canvas.

## Prerequisites

None stated in the syllabus.

## How it is graded

Grading is based on participation and response surveys (25%), article presentation and peer feedback (25%), pilot study and peer feedback (25%), and the final experimental proposal and peer feedback (25%).

## Workload

Heavy. Graded work includes weekly response surveys, an article presentation, a pilot study with data analysis, and a final experimental proposal with presentations and peer feedback.

DESCRIPTION
Businesses are increasingly using experimentation in a wide range of areas including product
pricing, marketing strategies, employee incentives and shaping consumer behavior. These
experiments can be particularly effective if the interventions tested incorporate insights from
behavioral economics. However, many companies currently lack the knowledge necessary to
take advantage of experimentation. An understanding of how to design and run a good
experiment will position students at the cutting edge of decision-making in business.
The goals of the course are for students to understand: (1) the role of experiments in business
(2) how insights from behavioral economics can be incorporated into experimental interventions
and (3) how to design and run an experiment in an organization.
We will begin by discussing the role of experiments in organizations and examining some recent
examples. Each student will be responsible for presenting a paper that describes an experiment
run in a company or non-profit organization.
Students will then work individually or in teams to develop an experimental question and design
a randomized experiment to test their question of interest. In the design phase, we will discuss
methods of experimental design as well as strategies for working with an organization to
implement experiments successfully. Students will run pilot studies to inform their designs. At the
end of the course, students will present a proposed experimental design and implementation
strategy.
About half of class time will consist of lecture. The other half will be spent workshopping concepts
from the lecture and developing students’ proposed experiments. The course is designed to give
students the practical tools they need to successfully run experiments in organizations.

ASSIGNMENTS
Response surveys and activities: Response surveys and activities based on the lecture and
reading are due:
• Mondays at 11:59 pm PT for online modules
• Fridays at 11:59 pm PT for in person modules
• The goal of the surveys is to track understanding and generate discussion. There is not
necessarily a “right answer”.
Article summary and analysis: Students will present a paper describing an experiment run in
an organization. The reading list includes example papers. You will prepare presentations
individually or with a partner. You will also provide feedback to presenters. You will provide
feedback individually.
Experimental proposal: Over the course of the class, you will work individually or in a group of
to develop a proposal for an experiment to be run in an organization. In module 9, students will
make a formal presentation of their experimental “pitch” as if presenting to the key decision maker
in the organization. Your grade will be based both on your final proposal and your development
of the proposal throughout the class. You will also provide peer feedback to the presentations of
your classmates. In module 10, you will submit your finalized proposal incorporating feedback you
received in your presentation.
Pilot study: Over the course of the class, students will design, run and analyze a pilot study to
provide initial feedback for their experimental proposals. In module 9, you will present the results
of your pilot study. You will also provide feedback to other groups’ pilot studies. You will work in
your experimental proposal group for the pilot study. In module 10, you will submit your finalized
pilot study results incorporating feedback you received in your presentation.

Supplemental reading:
Module 0: Glennerster and Takavarasha (GT), Running Randomized Evaluations, Chapter 2, modules 2.1-2.3 (pp. 24-47). These modules
discusses both experimental and non-experimental methods for measuring causal impact. At the end of the reading, you should understand what
causal impact is and be able to discuss the advantages and disadvantages of the different methods.
Module 0: Glennerster and Takavarasha (GT), Running Randomized Evaluations, Chapter 4, module 4.2 (pp. 108-120). This module
provides a critical overview of randomization. At the end of the reading, you should be able to apply the concepts in the reading to the evaluation
and design of experiments; in particular, choosing a level of randomization.
Module 6: Glennerster and Takavarasha (GT), Running Randomized Evaluations, Chapter 4, module 4.1 (pp. 98-108), 4.4-4.5 (pp. 140161). These modules discuss opportunities for randomization, randomization designs, and methods for randomizing participants. At the end of
the reading, you should be able to apply the concepts in the reading and implement randomizations, including understanding the difference
between a lottery design and an encouragement design, as well as the role of stratification (or blocking) in randomizations to achieve balance.
Module 7: Reading: Glennerster and Takavarasha (GT) Chapter 6, modules 6.1-6.5. These modules discuss statistical analysis and statistical
power. At the end of the reading, you should be able to apply the concepts to choosing appropriate sample sizes for experiments.
