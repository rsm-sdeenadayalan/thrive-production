# MGT 403R — Business Analytics for Managers

**4 units** · Rady MBA · offered FA26

## What this course covers

This course introduces students to the foundations of business analytics. It teaches
managers how to be comfortable exploring their data, how to create and use models to
make better decisions in complex settings, and how to use statistics to better
understand their business setting. The course covers uncertainty, decision analysis, and
optimization.

## What you should be able to do afterwards

- exploring and visualizing data
- conducting hypothesis tests
- building regression models
- interpreting regression results
- using simulation for decision-making
- optimizing resource allocation
- communicating analytical results to non-technical audiences
- making data-driven business decisions

## Topics covered

probability distributions, hypothesis testing, confidence intervals, regression analysis, decision analysis, sensitivity analysis, simulation, linear optimization, integer optimization, data visualization.

## Tools used

radiant, r, canvas.

## Prerequisites

None stated in the syllabus.

## How it is graded

Final project writeup 40%, final project presentation 5%, individual case quizzes and writeups 25%, individual homework assignment quizzes 30%.

## Workload

Heavy. Eight weekly assignments, five case quizzes with writeups, and a final project with report and presentation.

DESCRIPTION
In today’s increasingly digital economy, the availability of data is widespread and business success can
depend on smart, capable, and clever use of this data. Business analytics now drives ticket pricing, retail
assortments, movie recommendations, digital marketing campaigns, healthcare scheduling, crime
fighting, social media investments, capacity decisions and more. Today’s companies generate an
immense volume of transactional data, capturing trillions of bytes of information about their customers,
suppliers, and operations. Millions of networked sensors are also embedded in the physical world in
devices such as computers, mobile phones, smart energy meters, automobiles and industrial machines.
Further, in a digitized world, consumers communicate, browse, buy, share, and search, creating extensive
trails of valuable data.
Hence, analytics — the use of data with statistical and quantitative models to make better, data-driven
business decisions — has become important to gain competitive advantages. With all of these different
sources of data ranging from online sensors, email campaigns, to physical sensors, business analytics
is pervasive in any size business.
This importance of analytics means that all managers should be comfortable discussing, assessing, and
effectively conducting their own analyses. There is a significant shortage (and significant demand!) of
business analysts and project managers who are adept at effectively utilizing analytics to create
substantial value to an organization by solving impactful business problems.
This course introduces students to the foundations of business analytics. It teaches managers how to
be comfortable exploring their data, how to create and use models to make better decisions in complex
settings, and how to use statistics to better understand their business setting. Prior analytical experience
is not a prerequisite for this course, we will introduce all of the topics throughout this course, and students
of all different backgrounds have found the class useful and approachable.
OBJECTIVES
There are three main Units we cover in this course. Each Unit teaches you tools to answer key datadriven and quantitative questions. Each of the three units center around a central theme of addressing
and studying uncertainty. Data and business analytics allows us to better understand and deal with
uncertainty.

Unit 1: Uncertainty (& Randomness)
• Modeling randomness to understand what risks and potential outcomes you face.
• Understanding why data gives us confidence and interpreting such confidence.
• Making evidence-based business decisions (“hypothesis testing”).
o Is the evidence sufficient to convince us? Or is it just noise? [Key business question.]
• Use data to create predictions for the future (“regression”).
• Use regression to interpret the relationships between data.
o Understand the limitations of regression and how to address those limitations.
o Understand the limitations of causality interpretation.
Unit 2: Decision Analysis
In Unit 2, we build on the building blocks of uncertainty that we study in Unit 1 and utilize them to
structure our decision-making tools and process.
• Decisions when facing uncertain and probabilistic (“random”) future outcomes.
• Test the important of our assumptions in our models (“sensitivity analysis”)
• Use simulation to predict complex future outcomes (such as long-tail insurance risks).
Unit 3: Optimization
In optimization, we transition from modeling random outcomes to building tools to optimize decisions,
such as how many resources to allocate to different sectors or investments. Each use of resources has
tradeoffs, which we learn to use tools to quantitatively model and consider.
• Simulating possible futures, and using this simulation to make the optimal decision.
• Formalizing the resource tradeoffs you face in a business into an optimization model.
• Using optimization model to understand the best use of many scarce resources, when faced with
many tradeoffs.
• Advanced optimization models using integer and binary constraints.
All of these are key ways businesses and managers must think about their problems. This course will give
you ample experience in these fundamental ways of quantitative thinking.
RADIANT TOOL
The central tool we use in this course to perform analysis is Radiant.
The modern use of statistics and analytics heavily relies on statistical software to conduct analyses. “R” is
one of the most famous and is heavily used throughout all companies. However, using R requires being
comfortable with coding first.
To best teach analytical thinking, we use Radiant to give you the power of exploring your data, visualizing
it, and seeing analysis without needing to code. Radiant is built on-top of R, meaning it is free and it can
be used at any business that can use R — which again, almost all modern analytics teams support the
use of R.
Lastly and most importantly, Radiant is a valuable resume piece. This course will not only give you
comfort with “data visualization”, “business analytics”, “regression analysis”, and “linear optimization” —
you will also have experience with “R statistical analysis (via the Radiant interface)”.

CASES ASSIGNMENTS
There are 5 cases due this course in addition to weekly assignment quizzes.
• These cases consist of a slightly more in-depth analysis in a specific business and industry
setting. The cases provide helpful background on a relevant business problem and background
setting. The analyses for the cases will be slightly more difficult, however they can be solved
using the same tools you learned in each week’s class.
• Case Quiz: Each case assignment will include several quiz questions to test you on the analytical
aspects of the case. Similar to the assignment quizzes, these quizzes can also be submitted
twice.
• Case Writeup:
• Each case will also require you to submit a 1-2 page brief writeup on a few key aspects on each
case problem. In this writeup, you will be asked to write in non-technical language suitable for a
CEO, board member, or potential investor. Bullet points or short paragraphs are okay, but still, full
sentences are required. Consider each case writeup as a brief executive summary.
•

•
•

Specifically, you will cover:
(a) what are the background inputs you used for the analysis, and key assumptions,
(b) a high-level explanation of the kind of analysis you performed and the intuition behind it,
(c) discuss some high-level key takeaways from the analysis, and,
(d) importantly, as an open-ended discussion question, what might be some flaws in this analysis,
or assumptions you may want to question.
A detailed rubric and example of this will be provided in Week 2’s lecture.
The goal for these case writeups will be to practice with not just performing analytics in a realworld business setting, but being able to (1) communicate it in a non-technical way, and (2)
consider how it could be improved.

Language Models AI + ChatGPT POLICY
ChatGPT and large-language AI models have become popular in the recent years. They can answer simple
statistical questions. Modern statistical analysts and programmers can use these, however, they must also be
wary that they can make simple mistakes too.
If you find it helpful, you are welcome to try to use ChatGPT to offer advice or additional support on your
homework and cases. However, you must ultimately do the work on your own in Radiant and show the
supporting evidence. We expect each student to upload their own Radiant state (and supporting) files
to demonstrate their ability to perform the analysis themselves without AI support. And for the case
writing, you are expected to write any discussions and include your own Radiant analysis. The TA will be
particularly vigilent in their grading of any non-sequitur output that a ChatGPT-type model might output.
Furthermore, for the take-home project, you must write the report and answer the analytics questions on your
own. Given that this is an MBA course, the goal is primarily your education and growth, not a particular letter
grade. The final has been one of the best learning resources for students in the past, so I encourage you to
write and analyze the take-home project primarily on your own. The final exam also requires uploading your
own Radiant state file as supporting evidence.
