# MGT 409 — Leading in Networks and Organizations

Rady MBA · offered WI26 · _unit count not stated in the material supplied_

## What this course covers

This course explores major determinants of a firm's success or failure and how to manage
challenging organizational dynamics. Topics include social and professional networks,
organizational networks, equity and inclusion, organizational culture, innovation,
organizational design, corruption, scandals, and fostering change. It is taught through
in-class group exercises, case discussions, and evidence-based insights from behavioral
and management science.

## What you should be able to do afterwards

- analyzing organizational success and failure
- diagnosing organizational dynamics
- leveraging professional networks
- developing evidence-based solutions
- communicating insights
- conducting postmortem case analysis
- proposing change strategies
- leveraging professional networks for career and firm benefit
- diagnosing factors contributing to organizational success or failure
- analyzing real-world organizational challenges

## Topics covered

social and professional networks, organizational networks, information exchange in markets, equity and inclusion, organizational culture, innovation, organizational design, corruption, organizational scandals, organizational change.

DESCRIPTION
Why do some organizations succeed but others fail? And what can be done about it? The
answers to these questions are consequential to your professional success because, regardless
of career choice, people spend most of their lives as part of one organization or another. Building
on lessons from prior leadership and team management courses, MGT 409 explores major
determinants of a firm’s success or failure, and how to manage challenging organizational
dynamics. Topics include social and professional networks, organizational networks and
information exchange in markets, equity and inclusion, organizational culture and innovation,
organizational design, corruption, organizational scandals, and fostering change in firms.
Instructional methods. To translate theory into practice, this course is taught in an applied
manner. Class sessions are structured around group exercises that allow you to engage with
and experience relevant course concepts firsthand. Exercises are followed by instructorfacilitated group discussions of illustrative case studies and evidence-based insights generated
by behavioral and management scientists, which contextualize your experiences and ground
course concepts in real organizational settings. Due to the highly condensed nature of this class,
there is also time built-in to work on a mini capstone final project that will help you apply what
you learn in real time.
OBJECTIVES
The central learning objective is to provide you with an organizational strategy toolkit, which can
be used throughout your career. By the end of this course, you will be able to:
• Identify important characteristics of your social and professional network and understand
how to leverage your network to benefit your career and your firm
• Identify the major contributing factors to an organization’s success or failure, and
critically analyze/diagnose these processes in real organizations
• Develop evidence-based solutions to challenging organizational dynamics and
effectively communicate your insights and strategies

Class Policy for use of Generative AI: This policy applies to use of various new generative AI
tools that can generate text, images, etc., in response to prompts by users (e.g., ChatGPT,
BingChat, Bard, Midjourney, etc.). These new tools and programs have considerable potential
to streamline and augment our work. You are permitted to use these tools for this class, with
several caveats and conditions:
1. These tools should augment your work, not replace it. For instance, large language
models can provide help improving the quality of your writing, identify new sources, get
high-level summaries of complex ideas or terms, or pressure test your arguments for
logical inconsistencies. These tools can help you get started on idea, help overcome
writer’s block, or refine YOUR original ideas. These tools should not be used generate
content that you simply put your name on. This constitutes an academic integrity
violation, much like taking another individual’s work and misrepresenting it as your own
original work (see end of syllabus for details on UCSD’s academic integrity policy).
Moreover, such an approach is likely to lead to low quality responses and low scores
because these tools often produce rudimentary and superficial analysis.
2. If you use generative AI for an assignment, be transparent about if/how it was used in a
footnote or citation. Using generative AI tools without attributing credit will be treated as
comparable to failing to properly credit and cite/reference other people’s contributions
and sources used in your work.
3. Large language models have documented issues with hallucination and inaccuracy (e.g.,
generating fake citations, failing logical arguments, misrepresenting sources, etc.). You
are fully responsible for the content you submit in your assignments and will be graded
accordingly.
4. Overreliance on generative AI tools, specifically in the form of using it to “think” for you,
is shown to be associated with brain atrophy (see MIT Media Lab study here). The way
to avoid this is to engage in your own critical thinking first and then use generative AI
tools to help refine your original ideas. Keep this in mind!

Group Project: Real World Case Analysis (group grade). To demonstrate your ability to apply

*** I recognize this class schedule is a tight timeline for group projects! Note you will have time

Because students in this class can come from multiple cohorts, teams assignments will be based
on study groups but students from other cohorts will also be randomly assigned. Each team can
pick their own topic. I recommend using the following steps as a guide for approaching this group
project:
1. Pick an organizational failure event or challenge that you find interesting. Feel free
to interpret this broadly. Think about a firm that has faced and failed to overcome a
competitive challenge, failed in a major or meaningful way, or was embroiled in a
scandal. History is full of organizational failures, giving you many possible topics. There
is a list of possible topics on Canvas to give you inspiration, but you are not limited to
this list. All topics are subject to my final approval. The constraints on topic selection are:
• It must be an organizational failure and not a single person’s failure.
• It cannot already be covered in a case study discussed in class (see schedule
at the end of the syllabus for a list of cases being used in class).
• The topic must be well enough known that there is sufficient evidence to analyze.
• No two groups can do the same topic. Once you identify your topic, put it on the
sign-up list on Canvas to claim it.
Keep in mind that many organizational failures have had serious consequences for
people’s lives and livelihoods. Please be respectful of this when selecting your topic and
conducting your analysis.
2. Figure out what happened, why it happened, and what you would have done
differently. I recommend reviewing the final project grading rubric available on Canvas
to understand how your project will be evaluated. A compelling postmortem analysis
should address the following and be supported by reliable evidence:

DESCRIPTION
Why do some organizations succeed but others fail? And what can be done about it? The
answers to these questions are consequential to your professional success because, regardless
of career choice, people spend most of their lives as part of one organization or another. Building
on lessons from prior leadership and team management courses, MGT 409 explores major
determinants of a firm’s success or failure, and how to manage challenging organizational
dynamics. Topics include social and professional networks, organizational networks and
information exchange in markets, equity and inclusion, organizational culture and innovation,
organizational design, corruption, organizational scandals, and fostering change in firms.
Instructional methods. To translate theory into practice, this course is taught in an applied
manner. Class sessions are structured around group exercises that allow you to engage with
and experience relevant course concepts firsthand. Exercises are followed by instructorfacilitated group discussions of illustrative case studies and evidence-based insights generated
by behavioral and management scientists, which contextualize your experiences and ground
course concepts in real organizational settings.
OBJECTIVES
The central learning objective is to provide you with an organizational strategy toolkit, which can
be used throughout your career. By the end of this course, you will be able to:
• Identify important characteristics of your social and professional network and understand
how to leverage your network to benefit your career and your firm
• Identify the major contributing factors to an organization’s success or failure, and
critically analyze/diagnose these processes in real organizations
• Develop evidence-based solutions to challenging organizational dynamics and
effectively communicate your insights and strategies

Class Policy for use of Generative AI: This policy applies to use of various new generative AI
tools that can generate text, images, etc., in response to prompts by users (e.g., ChatGPT,
BingChat, Bard, Midjourney, etc.). These new tools and programs have considerable potential
to streamline and augment our work. You are permitted to use these tools for this class, with
several caveats and conditions:
1. These tools should augment your work, not replace it. For instance, large language
models can provide help improving the quality of your writing, identify new sources, get
high-level summaries of complex ideas or terms, or pressure test your arguments for
logical inconsistencies. These tools can help you get started on idea, help overcome
writer’s block, or refine YOUR original ideas. These tools should not be used generate
content that you simply put your name on. This constitutes an academic integrity violation,
much like taking another individual’s work and misrepresenting it as your own original
work (see end of syllabus for details on UCSD’s academic integrity policy). Moreover,
such an approach is likely to lead to low quality responses and low scores because these
tools often produce rudimentary and superficial analysis.
2. If you use generative AI for an assignment, be transparent about if/how it was used in a
footnote or citation. Using generative AI tools without attributing credit will be treated as
comparable to failing to properly credit and cite/reference other people’s contributions
and sources used in your work.
3. Large language models have documented issues with hallucination and inaccuracy (e.g.,
generating fake citations, failing logical arguments, misrepresenting sources, etc.). You
are fully responsible for the content you submit in your assignments and will be graded
accordingly.

Class Engagement (individual grade, 25 pts). Everyone’s learning experience benefits from
high-quality discussions and active engagement in class exercises, so we should all contribute
by participating in class. Your participation score is a holistic assessment that takes several
things into account:
• Attendance: Due to the number of in-class group exercises and discussions, this course
cannot accommodate a hybrid format (see course format section for more information).
Attending class in person is important for your and your classmates’ learning experience.
But do not come to class if you are sick! You will never be penalized for missing class
due to illness or other extenuating circumstances beyond your control. If you will be
missing a class, please reach out to me as soon as possible so we can work out an
alternative plan for you to earn participation credit.
• In-class participation: This includes preparing for, actively participating in, and
thoughtfully contributing to group discussions, group exercises, and class-wide
discussions. The quality of your contributions is more important than the quantity of your
contributions.
• Canvas discussion board participation: In-class participation is not the only way that
you can contribute. You can also participate by posting to the discussion boards on
Canvas. As an example, a good contribution would be posting a link to an article that
illustrates a relevant course concept and briefly commenting.
• Peer evaluations: You will be asked to complete a survey at the end of the quarter that
asks you about how you see each other as contributors and participants in class, which
I will use to augment my personal assessment. You will also receive credit for completing
these evaluations (see final project milestones).
Thought Questions (individual grade). There are 4 Thought Question responses due
throughout the quarter (10 points/each). The goal of this assignment is to reflect on your intuition,
educated opinions, and beliefs about handling organizational challenges, managing various
workplace situations, or making career decisions, and what information has led you to form these
opinions. For instance, you might be asked to consider a hypothetical workplace scenario and
what decisions you would make or how you would handle certain challenges. This helps you
draw direct links between course concepts and your personal experiences. This assignment
provides a foundation for discussing new evidence and learnings on these same topics during
class, which might support or challenge your current beliefs and opinions. When writing these
responses, I recommend keeping the following in mind:
• Responses should articulate a clear perspective/argument and be supported by
evidence. There are no incorrect stances or positions, but you are graded on how clearly
you articulate that position and the evidence you are drawing upon to justify that
perspective. You can incorporate traditional and anecdotal evidence like personal
experiences, learnings from this class or other courses, advice from peers or mentors,
evidence from reliable sources (e.g., news articles, articles written by experts, white
papers), etc.
• Responses are always due before the start of class.
• Responses should be concise and generative for in-class discussions (max of 300 words
in total). Excessively long responses will not receive high scores.
• Although you are always welcome to discuss course material with your classmates,
these responses must be written individually.

Exit Polls (individual grade). At the end of Classes 2-9, you will briefly reflect on what concepts
from that class session you found most interesting and what is still confusing, if anything. These
exit polls provide me with information about the class’s progress and what concepts need to be
reviewed. Reflecting at the end of a class session has also been shown to significantly improve
retention of the information. Answers should be 1-2 sentences and completed within 24 hours of
the end of the class session (each response is scored complete/incomplete).
Open-book Final Quiz (individual grade). During finals, you will complete an open-book, takehome online quiz. The goal of the quiz is to test your ability to analyze, apply, and draw
connections between key course concepts, rather than memorization of course content.
Additional details about the format of the quiz will be provided in class. Quiz responses must be
completed individually. The quiz questions will be available on Canvas for download, and you
will have 1 week to complete them (deadlines on Canvas). If there is a compelling reason you
cannot complete the quiz during the allotted time span, please reach out ASAP so we can come
up with an alternative plan.
Group Project: Real World Case Analysis (group grade, 60 pts). To demonstrate your ability
to apply course concepts and communicate evidence-based solutions, you will work on a team
to conduct a postmortem analysis of a real organizational challenge or failure event and propose
a do-over strategy. This final project includes a written memo and an in-class presentation. Your
team will be your study group, and each team can pick their topic. I recommend using the
following steps as a guide for approaching this group project:
1. Pick an organizational failure event or challenge that you find interesting. Feel free
to interpret this broadly. Think about a firm that has faced and failed to overcome a
competitive challenge, failed in a major or meaningful way, or was embroiled in a
scandal. History is full of organizational failures, giving you many possible topics. There
is a list of possible topics on Canvas to give you inspiration, but you are not limited to
this list. All topics are subject to my final approval. The constraints on topic selection are:
• It must be an organizational failure and not a single person’s failure.
• It cannot already be covered in a case study discussed in class (see schedule at
the end of the syllabus for a list of cases being used in class).
• The topic must be well enough known that there is sufficient evidence to analyze.
• No two groups can do the same topic. Once you identify your topic, put it on the
sign-up list on Canvas to claim it.
Keep in mind that many organizational failures have had serious consequences for
people’s lives and livelihoods. Please be respectful of this when selecting your topic and
conducting your analysis.
2. Figure out what happened, why it happened, and what you would have done
differently. I recommend reviewing the final project grading rubric available on Canvas
to understand how your project will be evaluated. A compelling postmortem analysis
should address the following and be supported by reliable evidence:
• Description/background context: What happened and why is it important for us to
understand?
• Analysis: What were the major contributing factors or catalysts to this failure event,
and why?

Proposed do-over strategy: Imagine that you have gone back in time and the firm
has hired your team as consultants to help them navigate this challenge or failure
event in real time. You are encouraged to approach this creatively. For instance,
depending on the type of failure event, you could imagine your team was hired just
before the failure occurred (i.e., you focus on preventing the failure) or after a failure
becomes public (i.e., you focus on improving the firm’s post-scandal response).
What are your strategic recommendations for how the firm should handle this
situation, or could have avoided it altogether? Why will your proposed strategy be
successful?

3. Tell us about your insights. Write up your analysis and proposed do-over strategy in
a memo and present your findings to class.
Written memo: This is a report of your evidence, analysis, insights, and do-over strategy.
You can be creative with formatting, and I recommend thinking about the most
compelling, informative, and persuasive structure to present this information to the firm’s
leadership. The memo should not be longer than 2,000 words (equivalent of ~8 pgs
double spaced with 12-pt font). Appendices with supplementary evidence, tables/figures,
references, brief abstracts, and title pages are excluded from the word count.
Excessively long memos will not receive high scores.
In-class presentation: Your group will have 10-15 minutes during Class 10 to present
your findings to your classmates (additional details provided in class). These
presentations are intended to be overviews and are not expected to cover everything
that is in your written memo. Think critically about what is most important.
Note: You will be learning course content relevant to your project through Class 9, which
is only a few days before the final presentations. I understand this is a time crunch and
do not expect Class 9 content to be integrated into the presentation (but you are
welcome to if you’d like). But you are given adequate time to integrate any/all relevant
course concepts for the written memo.
